How to stop fighting with your partner

Different day, different topic, same argument. Here is why it keeps coming back, how to stop it while it's happening, and a way out that doesn't need anyone to win.

Updated 29 October 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca7 min read
Short answer

Couples keep having the same fight when the topic (the dishes, the phone, the plans) isn't the real issue. Underneath is a need on each side, like feeling respected or feeling like a priority, that hasn't been heard yet. To stop it, take a break when either of you is flooded, then name the pattern together, find the need under the topic, repeat back each other's side, and agree on one small step you'll actually try.

Why you keep having the same argument

Most repeating arguments have two layers. On top is the topic: who forgot to text, who's on their phone, whose turn it was. Underneath is the thing that actually hurts. As long as you only argue about the top layer, you can "solve" it a dozen times and still end up in the same place next week.

It's the same whether it's your boyfriend, your girlfriend, your husband or your wife. In a long marriage, the topic may change over the years while the need underneath stays exactly the same. A few examples of how the layers often line up:

  • "You're always on your phone" often means "I miss you, and I don't feel chosen right now."
  • "You never plan anything" often means "I'm tired of carrying us on my own."
  • "Why didn't you text me back?" often means "When I don't hear from you, I start to feel like I don't matter."
  • "You're overreacting" often means "I feel blamed, and I don't know how to fix it."

Relationship researcher John Gottman found that most conflicts between partners, around two-thirds in his studies, are "perpetual": they come from lasting differences in personality or needs, so they never fully go away. That sounds bleak, but it's freeing. The goal isn't to win the argument once and for all. It's to be able to talk about it without hurting each other every time.

Signs you're stuck in a loop

  • You can predict, almost word for word, what each of you will say next.
  • The fight starts about something small and ends about something much bigger.
  • "You always" and "you never" show up early.
  • It ends when someone gives up or walks away, not when something is agreed.
  • A day later, neither of you could say what you actually decided.

How to stop a fight while it's happening

When your heart is racing, it's very hard to listen, however good your intentions. If either of you feels flooded, the most useful thing you can do is stop, on purpose, and say when you'll come back. Take at least twenty minutes and do something that genuinely settles you: a walk, a shower, music. Don't spend the break rehearsing your case.

  • "I'm too wound up to listen properly. Can we pause and pick this up at eight?"Names the pause and the return, so it doesn't feel like walking out.
  • "I want to get this right with you, and I can't right now. Give me twenty minutes."Makes clear the break is for the relationship, not against your partner.

Five steps to break the loop

1. Name the loop, not the person

Talk about the pattern as something that happens to both of you, not as one person's fault. It turns you into two people looking at a problem, instead of two people looking at each other.

  • "I think we keep having the same fight about plans. Can we look at it together, when we're not in the middle of it?"

2. Pick a calm time and one topic

Come back to it when you're both rested, not at midnight or in the car. One issue per conversation. Bringing in old examples turns a conversation into a trial.

3. Find the need under the topic

Each of you answers the same question: what is the part of this that hurts most? Not what the other person did, but what it made you feel or fear. This is usually where the real conversation starts.

  • "When you didn't text, the part that hurt was feeling like an afterthought."
  • "When you brought it up in front of your sister, I felt embarrassed, and I shut down."

4. Repeat back before you answer

Before you defend yourself, say back what you heard, in your own words, and ask if you got it right. Keep going until the other person says "yes, that's it." It feels slow. It's also the fastest way to stop someone from repeating themselves louder.

  • "So what you're saying is that you felt alone at the table, and it wasn't really about the time. Is that right?"

5. Agree on one small, specific step

Not a promise to "communicate better." One concrete thing each of you will try, and a day to check in on how it went. Small steps get done. Big promises turn into the next argument.

  • "If plans change, I'll text before, not after. Can we check in on Sunday about how it's going?"

When you both feel strongly

Sometimes there isn't a hidden misunderstanding. You simply want different things: more time with friends and more time together, saving and spending, your family and theirs. In that case, stop looking for who's right and look for what each of you needs to feel okay. A compromise built on both needs ("two evenings out, and Sunday is ours") lasts much longer than a decision one of you gave in to.

What makes it worse

  • Arguing by text when it's heated. Tone gets lost, and screenshots last forever.
  • Bringing in a referee. Asking friends who's right gets you allies, not understanding.
  • Aiming for a verdict. If one of you "wins," the other usually stops listening, and the fight comes back in a different outfit.

When it's more than a loop. If your arguments involve fear, threats, control or any kind of violence, these steps aren't the answer. Please reach out to local emergency services or a qualified professional. And if you've been stuck in the same place for a long time, a couples therapist can help in ways an app or an article can't.

Common questions

Is it normal to keep fighting about the same thing?

Yes. Relationship research suggests most conflicts between partners are recurring, because they come from lasting differences in personality or needs. What matters is not whether the topic comes back, but whether you can talk about it without hurting each other.

How do you stop a fight in the moment?

Call a pause before it escalates, and say when you'll come back: "I'm too wound up to listen. Can we pick this up at eight?" Take at least twenty minutes to calm down, then return to it.

What if my partner won't talk about it?

Choose a calm moment, keep it about one specific situation, and start with how you felt rather than what they did. If they still shut down every time, that pattern itself is worth raising, possibly with a couples therapist.

When should we see a couples therapist?

When the same problem has lasted a long time despite trying, after a betrayal, when one of you is struggling with your mental health, or whenever there's fear or control in the relationship.
