My partner shuts down during arguments

You want to talk it through. They go quiet, stare at their phone, or leave the room. The harder you push, the further away they get. Here's what's usually going on, and what to say tonight.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca6 min read
Short answer

Most people who shut down in an argument aren't punishing you. They're overwhelmed: their body is in fight-or-flight, and they can't think or speak clearly, so they withdraw. Pushing harder makes it worse. Instead, agree on a real break of at least twenty minutes with a set time to come back, and when you do, start softly with one specific thing.

Why people shut down

Relationship researchers call it stonewalling, and the usual cause is flooding: during conflict, heart rate and stress hormones rise until the person can no longer take in what's being said. From the outside it looks like not caring. From the inside it feels like "if I say anything right now, it'll go badly."

Common reasons someone floods faster than their partner:

  • They grew up where conflict was loud or dangerous, so silence was the safest option.
  • They feel they always lose the verbal back-and-forth, so they stop playing.
  • They're afraid of saying something they'll regret, and silence is how they protect the relationship.
  • The argument feels like an attack on who they are, not a complaint about one thing.

Shutting down vs. the silent treatment

They look the same but aren't. Shutting down is involuntary: the person is overwhelmed and usually comes back once they've calmed down. The silent treatment is a tactic: silence used for days to punish or control, often until you apologise. If it's the second, read red flags in texts, and if you feel afraid, talk to someone you trust or a professional.

What to say tonight

When you notice them going quiet, name it gently and offer a break with a return time:

  • "I can see this is getting too much. Let's stop for now. Can we try again at eight?"Takes the pressure off and promises you'll come back.
  • "I'm not trying to win this. I just want to understand what's going on for you."Lowers the stakes.
  • "You don't have to answer now. Can you tell me when you're ready?"Gives them control over the timing.

When you come back together, start with one specific moment and how you felt, not everything at once:

  • "When the plans changed last minute, I felt like I didn't matter. Can we talk about that one thing?"
  • "What was it like for you when I raised my voice earlier?"

What tends to make it worse:

  • "Say something!"Demands words they can't find yet.
  • "You always do this. You never talk to me."Turns a moment into a verdict on their character.
  • Following them from room to roomFeels like being cornered, which floods them more.

How to take a break that actually works

  1. Agree on it in advance, on a calm day: "If either of us is flooded, we can call a time-out."
  2. Make it at least twenty minutes. It takes that long for the body to settle.
  3. Don't rehearse the argument during the break. Walk, shower, breathe, read.
  4. Always come back at the time you agreed. A break without a return is just avoidance.

Common questions

Why does my partner shut down when I try to talk about problems?

Usually because they're overwhelmed, not because they don't care. When stress rises during conflict, some people can't think or speak clearly and withdraw to stop things getting worse.

Is shutting down the same as the silent treatment?

No. Shutting down is involuntary overwhelm and usually passes once they calm down. The silent treatment is silence used deliberately, often for days, to punish or control.

How long should a break during an argument be?

At least twenty minutes, so your body can calm down, with a clear time to come back and continue.

What should I not do when my partner shuts down?

Don't demand an answer, follow them around, or list everything they always do. Those make the overwhelm worse. Offer a break and a time to return instead.
