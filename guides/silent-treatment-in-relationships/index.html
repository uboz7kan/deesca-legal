My partner gives me the silent treatment

No reply. One-word answers. A house that feels cold for days. The silent treatment can hurt more than shouting, because you can't argue with nothing. Here's what it means and what you can do.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca7 min read
Short answer

The silent treatment means deliberately withdrawing communication, often to show anger or to make you give in. A short pause to calm down is healthy; silence used for days to punish or control is not. Calmly name what's happening, say you're ready to talk when they are, and don't chase or over-apologise. If it's a regular pattern used to control you, treat it as a serious red flag.

What the silent treatment is, and isn't

Taking space

"I'm too upset to talk right now. Can we talk tonight?" It's said out loud, it has an end, and they come back. That's healthy, and often wise.

The silent treatment

No explanation, no end date. You're left guessing what you did, and it usually stops only when you apologise or give in. That's a power move, even when it isn't fully conscious.

There's also a third thing: shutting down mid-argument because they're overwhelmed. It looks similar, but it passes once they calm down. More on partners who shut down.

Why people use it

  • They don't know how to say they're angry, so they show it instead.
  • It's what their family did. Silence was how conflict worked growing up.
  • They want you to feel how hurt they are.
  • It works: it ends with you apologising, so it gets repeated.
  • Control. In some relationships it's used on purpose to punish and keep the other person anxious.

Researchers who study the "demand-withdraw" pattern, where one partner pushes to talk and the other pulls away, have found it consistently linked to lower satisfaction for both people. The silence doesn't just hurt the person on the receiving end.

What to do

  1. Name it, once, calmly. Don't interrogate or plead.
  2. Leave the door open. Make it clear you want to talk and will be there when they're ready.
  3. Don't chase. Twenty messages in a row tends to make the silence last longer, not shorter.
  4. Don't apologise for something you didn't do just to end it. That teaches the silence that it works.
  5. Look after yourself. See a friend, go for a walk. Your day doesn't have to stop.
  6. Talk about the pattern later, when things are calm, not in the middle of it.

What to say

  • "I can tell you're upset with me. I'd really like to talk about it whenever you're ready."Names it without blame and leaves the door open.
  • "I'm not going to keep messaging. I'm here when you want to talk."Stops the chase, stays warm.
  • "When we don't speak for days, I feel shut out and anxious. Can we agree that if one of us needs space, we say so and say when we'll talk?"For the calm conversation afterwards.

What tends to make it worse:

  • "Fine, be like that."Escalates into a silence contest.
  • "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, just talk to me."Rewards the silence and leaves the real issue untouched.
  • Giving the silent treatment backTwo people waiting each other out rarely ends well.

The silent treatment over text

Being left on "read" is its own kind of painful. Send one short, calm message, then put the phone down: "I can see you're upset. I'm not going to keep texting, but I'd like to talk tonight if you're up for it." If you're not sure whether a message you're about to send will make it worse, Deesca's Red Flag Check reads a chat screenshot, names patterns like guilt-tripping or the silent treatment, and suggests three calm ways to reply. When the silent treatment is emotional abuse

It's more than a bad habit when it happens often, lasts for days, is used to punish you or force you to give in, comes with threats or contempt, or leaves you constantly anxious about what will set it off. In those situations, please don't try to fix it with better wording. Talk to someone you trust or a qualified professional.

If you don't feel safe, contact local emergency services or a domestic abuse helpline in your country.

Common questions

What does it mean when your partner gives you the silent treatment?

Usually that they're angry or hurt and are showing it by withdrawing instead of saying it. Sometimes it's a habit from childhood; sometimes it's used, consciously or not, to make the other person give in.

Is the silent treatment emotional abuse?

It can be. A short break to calm down is healthy. Silence used repeatedly, for days, to punish, control or make you anxious is a recognised form of emotional abuse.

How long is too long for the silent treatment?

Any silence without an explanation or an end point is a problem. A few hours to cool off is normal; days of being ignored is not.

Should I ignore someone giving me the silent treatment?

Don't chase and don't retaliate. Say once, calmly, that you're ready to talk when they are, then get on with your day and talk about the pattern when things are calm.
