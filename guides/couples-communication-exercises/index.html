10 communication exercises for couples

Knowing you should "communicate better" doesn't help much in the moment. These exercises give you a structure to follow instead. Most take ten minutes or less, and none need a therapist in the room.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca8 min read
Short answer

The most useful communication exercises for couples slow conversations down and make sure each person feels understood before anyone responds. Start with the speaker-listener technique for hard topics, a daily ten-minute check-in for connection, and a weekly "state of us" talk to catch problems early. Pick one or two, practise when you're calm, and use them when things get heated.

The 10 exercises

1. The speaker-listener technique

Developed by relationship researchers at the University of Denver, this is the classic for hard conversations. Use any object as "the floor": a pen, a remote.

  1. Whoever holds it speaks, in short chunks, about their own feelings.
  2. The listener only paraphrases what they heard. No rebuttals, no solutions.
  3. The speaker confirms or corrects. Then the object passes over.

It feels slow and awkward. That's the point: it makes escalation almost impossible.

2. Mirror, validate, empathise

A three-step listening response. Mirror: "So what you're saying is…" Validate: "That makes sense, because…" (you don't have to agree, only see their logic). Empathise: "I imagine that felt…" Use it when one of you feels unheard. More on feeling unheard.

3. "I feel… when… I need…"

Rewrite a complaint before you say it. "You're always on your phone" becomes "I feel lonely when we're on our phones at dinner. I need some time where we're actually talking." Practise by each rewriting three old complaints together.

4. The daily ten minutes

Ten minutes a day, phones away, no logistics. Each person talks about their day; the other just listens and asks one question. It's simple, and it's where most couples first notice a difference.

5. The weekly check-in

Twenty to thirty minutes, same time each week. What went well between us, what was hard, anything unsaid, and what each of you needs next week. Relationship researcher John Gottman recommends a version he calls a "State of the Union" meeting. Questions for a weekly check-in.

6. The agreed time-out

Agree in advance, while calm, on a word that means "I need a break". The rule: whoever calls it says when they'll come back, takes at least 20 minutes to calm down, and then actually comes back. Rules for fighting fair.

7. Both sides on paper

For a recurring argument, each person writes their side separately: what happened, how it felt, what they need. Swap and read. Seeing the other side written down, without tone of voice, often shows that you want more of the same thing than it seemed.

8. The dream behind the conflict

Take turns answering, without debate: "What does this issue mean to me? Is there a hope or a fear behind my position? Where does it come from in my life?" Recurring fights are often about something much bigger than the topic. Why the same argument keeps coming back.

9. The appreciation round

Each night, or a few times a week, each person names one specific thing they appreciated about the other that day. Specific matters: "Thanks for making coffee when I was rushing" lands far better than "you're great".

10. New questions

Once a week, ask each other a question you've never asked. "What's something you wanted to be when you were ten?" "What are you looking forward to this year?" Curiosity keeps you from becoming roommates. When you feel like roommates.

Making them stick

  • Practise when you're calm. An exercise you've never tried won't come to mind mid-fight.
  • Start with one. Doing all ten at once becomes homework, and homework gets dropped.
  • Agree on it together. Springing a technique on your partner in an argument can feel like a trick.
  • Expect awkwardness. It usually fades within a few tries.

Common questions

What is the best communication exercise for couples?

For hard conversations, the speaker-listener technique, because it stops interruptions and makes each person feel understood before anyone responds. For everyday closeness, a daily ten-minute check-in.

How can couples practise communication at home?

Pick one exercise, such as the daily ten minutes or a weekly check-in, agree on a time, and practise when you're calm so it's familiar when things get heated.

Do communication exercises work if only one partner tries?

Partly. Listening better and rewriting complaints as "I feel… I need…" changes how conversations go even if only one person does it, but the exercises work best when both agree to them.

What is the speaker-listener technique?

A structured exercise where one person speaks while holding an object, the other only paraphrases what they heard, and then the roles swap. It slows arguments down and prevents interruptions.
