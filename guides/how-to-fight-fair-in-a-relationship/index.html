How to fight fair in a relationship: 10 rules

Every couple argues. Fair fights end with both of you understanding a bit more. Unfair ones end with both of you hurt and nothing solved. These are rules you can agree on together, on a calm day, before the next one.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca6 min read
Short answer

Fighting fair means arguing about one issue at a time, using "I" statements instead of attacks, never using contempt or insults, taking a break when either of you is flooded, and ending with something agreed. The rules work best when you set them together on a calm day and both agree how to call a time-out.

10 rules for fighting fair

  1. One topic at a time. The fight is about tonight's plans, not everything since 2023.
  2. Start softly. How an argument starts usually predicts how it ends. "Can we talk about the weekend? I'm feeling a bit left out" beats "You never think about me."
  3. Talk about yourself, not their character. "I felt hurt" instead of "you're selfish."
  4. No contempt. No eye-rolling, mocking, sarcasm or insults. Of all the habits couples researchers have studied, contempt is one of the most damaging.
  5. No "always" or "never". They turn a moment into a verdict, and the other person will argue the exception.
  6. Listen to understand, not to reply. Before answering, say back what you heard.
  7. Either of you can call a time-out. If someone is flooded, stop for at least twenty minutes and agree when you'll come back.
  8. No threats. Not of leaving, not of breaking up, not to win a point.
  9. No fighting in front of others, or over text when it's getting heated.
  10. End with something. One thing you agree on, or a time to continue. Not just silence.

The four habits that make fights unfair

The Gottman Institute's research identified four patterns that predict trouble in relationships. Each has a fairer alternative:

HabitSounds likeTry instead
Criticism"You're so lazy.""I need help with the kitchen tonight."
ContemptEye-rolling, mocking, "wow, genius."Say what you appreciate, even mid-argument.
Defensiveness"It's not my fault, you…"Take responsibility for one part.
StonewallingGoing silent, walking away."I need a break. Let's talk at eight."

How to agree on your rules

  1. Pick a calm moment, not right after a fight.
  2. Each of you names the one thing the other does in fights that hurts most.
  3. Turn each into a rule you both follow: not "you stop yelling" but "neither of us raises our voice."
  4. Agree a time-out word or signal, and that it's always respected.
  5. Write the list down. Some couples keep it on their phones.

When a rule gets broken

It will. The point isn't perfection, it's repair. Call it gently ("I think we just broke our no-always rule"), pause if needed, and go back to the one topic. Afterwards, an apology for breaking the rule matters more than who was right. How to apologize so it lands.

Common questions

What are the rules for fighting fair in a relationship?

Stick to one topic, start softly, use "I" statements, avoid contempt, "always" and "never", listen before replying, allow time-outs, make no threats, and end with something agreed.

What are the four horsemen in relationships?

Criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling: four patterns the Gottman Institute found predict relationship trouble. Each has a gentler alternative.

How long should a time-out during a fight be?

At least twenty minutes, so both of you can calm down, with an agreed time to come back to the conversation.

Is it okay to argue over text?

Small logistics, yes. Anything emotional or heated is better in person or on a call, where tone and faces help.
