My husband yells at me

A small disagreement, and suddenly he's shouting. You go quiet, or you shout back, and either way nothing gets solved. Here's why it happens, what to do in the moment, and when it's more than a temper.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca7 min read
Short answer

Yelling usually means someone is flooded (too overwhelmed to think clearly) and has never learned another way to handle anger. In the moment, don't shout back: say calmly that you'll talk when voices are down, and take a break. Later, when it's calm, talk about the pattern and agree what happens next time. If the yelling comes with insults, threats, intimidation or fear, it's verbal abuse, not a communication problem, and your safety comes first.

If you're afraid of your partner, or the yelling comes with threats, breaking things, blocking you from leaving or any physical harm, this guide isn't enough. Contact local emergency services or a domestic abuse helpline in your country.

Why some people yell

  • Flooding. When the body goes into fight-or-flight, the heart races and the thinking brain goes offline. Volume rises before the person notices.
  • It's what they grew up with. In some families, shouting is just how disagreements sounded.
  • Stress from elsewhere. Work, money or tiredness spill into small arguments.
  • Feeling unheard. Some people get louder because they feel nobody is listening. More on feeling unheard.
  • It works for them. If yelling ends arguments in their favour, it gets repeated.
  • Control. In some relationships, yelling is used deliberately to intimidate.

Understanding why doesn't make it okay. It helps you work out which kind of problem you're dealing with.

What to do in the moment

  1. Don't match the volume. Two raised voices almost always escalate.
  2. Name it once, calmly. "I want to hear you, but I can't while you're shouting."
  3. Call a break. "I'm going to take twenty minutes. Then I'll come back and we can talk." Then actually leave the room.
  4. Calm yourself too. A few minutes of slow breathing helps your body come down, not just theirs.
  5. Come back when you said you would. A break that never ends feels like being shut out.

What to say

  • "I want to talk about this, but not like this. Let's take a break and try again in twenty minutes."Sets a boundary and a return time.
  • "When you shout, I stop being able to hear what you're saying. I just want it to stop."Explains the effect without insulting him.
  • "Can we agree that if either of us starts yelling, we stop and take a break?"For the calm conversation later.

What tends to make it worse:

  • Shouting backRaises the stakes on both sides.
  • "Calm down!"Almost never calms anyone down.
  • Apologising just to make it stopTeaches that yelling works.

The conversation afterwards

The real work happens when you're both calm, ideally the next day. Talk about the pattern, not the last fight: how it feels when he yells, what you need instead, and what you'll both do next time things heat up. Agreeing on a break word in advance helps a lot. Rules for fighting fair.

If he takes it seriously, apologises without blaming you, and the yelling becomes less frequent, that's a temper problem that's being worked on. If nothing changes, or it's always somehow your fault, that tells you something important too.

When yelling is verbal abuse

A temper problemVerbal abuse
Raised voice about the issueInsults, name-calling, put-downs
Rare, in heated momentsFrequent, often over small things
Genuine apology and effort to changeBlames you for "making" him yell
You disagree and feel safeYou're afraid of his reaction
Stops when you take a breakFollows you, blocks the door, breaks things

If the right-hand column sounds familiar, please don't try to fix it with better wording. Talk to someone you trust, a qualified professional, or a domestic abuse helpline. Couples counseling is usually not recommended where there's abuse; individual support is the safer first step.

Common questions

Is it normal for a husband to yell at his wife?

Raised voices happen in many relationships during heated moments, but regular yelling isn't healthy. Yelling that includes insults, threats or makes you afraid is verbal abuse.

How do I get my husband to stop yelling at me?

Don't shout back. Say calmly you'll talk when voices are down, take a break, and later agree together on what happens when either of you starts yelling. Real change depends on him taking it seriously.

When is yelling considered verbal abuse?

When it's frequent, includes insults or threats, is used to intimidate or control you, or leaves you afraid of your partner's reactions.

Why does my partner yell over small things?

Often because of stress from elsewhere, being quickly overwhelmed, or growing up where shouting was normal. Sometimes it's because yelling has worked to end arguments. None of these make it okay, but they point to different solutions.
