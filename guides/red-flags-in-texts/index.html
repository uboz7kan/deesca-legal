Red flags in texts: 9 patterns to notice

Some messages leave you feeling guilty, confused or small, and it's hard to say why. Here's what the most common unhealthy patterns look like in a chat, why they matter, and a calm way to reply.

Updated 29 October 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca8 min read
Short answer

The texting red flags worth noticing are patterns, not one-off bad messages: guilt-tripping, blaming you for their reactions, denying things they said, going silent to punish, passive-aggression, dismissing your feelings, keeping score, intense love-bombing early on, and controlling check-ins. One harsh text on a bad day isn't a red flag. The same move again and again, especially when it leaves you doubting yourself, is.

The 9 patterns
  1. Guilt-tripping
  2. Blame-shifting
  3. Denying what happened
  4. The silent treatment
  5. Passive-aggression
  6. Dismissing your feelings
  7. Keeping score
  8. Love-bombing
  9. Controlling check-ins

A pattern, not a message

Everyone sends a bad text sometimes: tired, stressed, or just badly worded. Before you label a message, ask two questions. Does this happen again and again? And after these conversations, do you usually end up apologising, doubting your memory, or feeling like you're "too much"? If the answer to both is yes, it's worth taking seriously.

The 9 patterns

01Guilt-tripping

They text"If you actually cared about us, you'd cancel."

Your ordinary plans get turned into a test of your love. You end up choosing between your own life and proving you care.

You could reply"I do care about us. I'm still going to dinner, and I'd love to see you after."

02Blame-shifting

They text"I only yelled because you pushed me. You know how I get."

Their reaction becomes your fault. Over time you start managing their mood instead of talking about what actually happened.

You could reply"I'm happy to talk about what I did. I'm not okay being blamed for how you reacted."

03Denying what happened

They text"I never said that. You're imagining things again."

Everyone misremembers sometimes. The red flag is when your memory is regularly treated as unreliable, until you start doubting yourself. This is often what people mean by gaslighting.

You could reply"We might remember it differently. What I heard was that I'm too sensitive, and that hurt."

04The silent treatment

They text"Fine." Then nothing for two days.

Needing space after a fight is healthy. Disappearing without saying when you'll talk, as a way to punish, isn't. It leaves the other person chasing.

You could reply"I can tell you're upset. I'm here when you want to talk. Can we pick a time?"

05Passive-aggression

They text"Do whatever you want. You always do."

It sounds like permission, but it's a complaint in disguise. You're left guessing what they actually want, and blamed either way.

You could reply"It sounds like you're not okay with it. Tell me what you'd actually like."

06Dismissing your feelings

They text"You're overreacting. It's not a big deal."

Your feelings get ruled out of order before they're heard. Repeated often enough, you stop bringing things up at all.

You could reply"It might not be big for you, but it is for me. Can you hear me out?"

07Keeping score

They text"After what you did last summer, you don't get to be upset."

An old mistake becomes a permanent trump card, so nothing current can be discussed on its own terms.

You could reply"I'd like to talk about today. We can talk about last summer separately if it still hurts."

08Love-bombing

They text, a week in"I've never felt like this about anyone. You're my soulmate. Delete your apps."

Intense devotion very early, often followed by pressure or sudden coldness. It's common in the texting stage of dating, and it can make normal boundaries feel like betrayal.

You could reply"I'm really enjoying getting to know you. I'd like to take it a bit slower."

09Controlling check-ins

They text"Why aren't you answering? Send me a photo of who you're with."

Wanting to hear from someone is normal. Demanding proof of where you are, who you're with, or instant replies is about control, not care.

You could reply"I'm out with friends and I'll text when I'm home. I'm not going to send photos."

When it's not a texting problem. Threats, including threats to hurt you or themselves, monitoring your phone or location, or being afraid of how someone will react are not something to fix with a better reply. Please talk to someone you trust, and contact local emergency services or a domestic abuse helpline if you feel unsafe.

What healthy texting looks like

  • They can say "I'm upset" and also when they'll talk about it.
  • They apologise for specific things, without "but".
  • Your plans with friends aren't treated as a threat.
  • Disagreements end with something agreed, not with someone giving up.
  • After a hard conversation, you feel heard, even if you didn't get your way.

What to do if you see these patterns

Start by noticing how often it happens, and how you feel afterwards. If it's occasional, raise it calmly, about one specific message, when you're both calm: "When you said I'm imagining things, I felt like you didn't believe me." If it keeps happening, or you feel smaller over time, talk to a friend you trust or a professional. You don't need to be certain something is "abuse" to deserve support.

Common questions

Is bad or dry texting a red flag?

Not by itself. Some people are simply slow or brief over text. It matters when slowness is used to punish, or when they're warm and responsive one day and cold the next, in a way that keeps you anxious.

Is texting too much a red flag?

Frequent texting can just be enthusiasm. It becomes a concern when it comes with pressure: expecting instant replies, demanding to know where you are, or getting upset when you're busy.

What are red flags in the texting stage of dating?

Very intense attention early on, pressure to commit or delete apps, guilt when you're busy, and hot-and-cold behaviour that keeps you guessing.

Can one message be a red flag?

Rarely. Most of these are about patterns. A single threat is the exception: take threats seriously the first time.
