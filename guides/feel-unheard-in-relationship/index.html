I feel unheard in my relationship

You've said it calmly. You've said it loudly. You've said it in a long text. And somehow you still end up feeling like you're talking to a wall. Here's why that happens, and how to say it so it lands.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca7 min read
Short answer

Feeling unheard usually isn't about volume or wording. It happens when your partner responds to your words (by fixing, defending or explaining) instead of to the feeling underneath. Ask for one specific kind of listening before you start ("I don't need a fix, I just need you to get it"), keep to one issue, and ask them to say back what they heard. If you've tried that many times and nothing changes, the problem is the pattern, not your phrasing.

Signs you feel unheard

  • You repeat the same point in every argument, because it never seems to register.
  • They jump straight to solutions, or to why you're wrong, before you've finished.
  • Conversations about your feelings end up being about their feelings.
  • You've started rehearsing what you'll say, or not bothering to say it at all.
  • They agree in the moment, then nothing changes.
  • You feel lonely in the relationship even when you're together.

Why it happens

Most people who don't listen well aren't trying to hurt anyone. These are the usual reasons:

  • They hear criticism, not a feeling. "I feel alone" lands as "you're a bad partner", so they defend instead of listen. More on defensiveness.
  • They're fixers. Offering a solution is how they show care. To you it feels like being brushed off.
  • They're overwhelmed. In a heated moment some people stop taking anything in. More on shutting down.
  • Too many issues at once. When five complaints arrive together, people answer the easiest one and miss the real one.
  • Bad timing. Raising it while they're tired, distracted or on their phone almost guarantees half-listening.
  • They don't care to understand. Rarer, but real. If your feelings are routinely mocked or dismissed, that's contempt, not a listening problem.

Relationship researcher John Gottman found that couples who stay together respond to each other's small bids for attention far more often than couples who split up. Feeling unheard is often hundreds of tiny missed moments, not one big conversation.

What to do

  1. Pick your moment. Ask "Is now a good time to talk about something that's been on my mind?" A yes buys you real attention.
  2. Say what kind of listening you need. "I don't need you to fix it. I just want you to understand how this feels for me." Most fixers will happily switch modes when told.
  3. One issue only. Write the others down for another day.
  4. Lead with the feeling and the need, not the accusation. "I feel invisible when plans change without me, and I need to be asked."
  5. Ask them to say it back. "Can you tell me what you heard?" It sounds formal, but it's the quickest way to find where the message got lost.
  6. Then listen to them, properly. People find it much easier to hear you once they feel heard themselves.

What to say

  • "I don't need a solution right now. I just need you to hear me."Tells a fixer exactly what to do.
  • "Can you tell me what you heard me say? I want to make sure it came out right."Takes the blame out of checking.
  • "I keep bringing this up because it still matters to me, not because I want to fight."For the issue that keeps coming back.
  • "When I'm talking and you're on your phone, I feel like I don't matter. Can we put them away for this?"Specific, not "you never listen".

What tends to make it worse:

  • "You never listen to me.""Never" invites a debate about whether it's true.
  • "Forget it, it doesn't matter."It does matter, and now it's buried.
  • Raising every unheard thing at onceThey'll answer the smallest one.

Hearing both sides

When it's more than a listening problem

If your feelings are routinely mocked, you're told you're "too sensitive" whenever you raise something, or you've stopped sharing because of how they react, that's a pattern worth taking seriously. Common questions

Why do I feel unheard in my relationship?

Usually because your partner responds to the words, by fixing, defending or explaining, instead of the feeling underneath. Timing, raising several issues at once and defensiveness all make it worse.

How do I get my partner to listen to me?

Pick a calm moment, ask for the kind of listening you need, keep to one issue, lead with the feeling and need rather than blame, and ask them to say back what they heard.

Is feeling unheard a reason to break up?

On its own, usually not. It's one of the most common relationship complaints and often improves with better timing and structure. It's more serious if your feelings are regularly mocked or dismissed, or nothing changes despite repeated, calm attempts.

What is it called when your partner doesn't listen to you?

There's no single term. Researchers talk about "turning away" from bids for attention, defensiveness and stonewalling. When it's paired with dismissing your feelings as invalid, people often call it emotional invalidation.
