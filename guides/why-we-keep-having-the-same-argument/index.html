Why do we keep having the same argument?

Different day, different trigger, same fight. The dishes, the phone, his mum, her friends. If you can predict how it ends before it starts, this guide is for you.

Updated 2 November 2026By Utku, founder of Deesca7 min read
Short answer

You keep having the same argument because you're arguing about the topic, not about what's underneath it. Recurring fights are usually about a need that isn't being met on each side, like feeling respected, feeling like a priority, or feeling free. Until both needs are named and heard, any solution to the surface problem won't hold, and the fight comes back in a new outfit.

First: this is normal

The Gottman Institute's long-term research on couples found that most relationship conflicts, around two thirds, are perpetual: they come from lasting differences in personality or values, and they never fully go away. Happy couples have them too. The difference is that they can talk about them without it turning into a battle, and with some humour and warmth.

So the goal isn't to never have this argument again. It's to have it differently: shorter, kinder, and with something actually agreed at the end.

Five reasons the same fight keeps coming back

1. You're fighting about the example, not the need

"You left the dishes again" is rarely about dishes. Underneath it might be "I feel like I'm carrying this house alone." His "you're on my case the second I walk in" might really be "I never feel good enough here." Two needs, both reasonable, both invisible.

2. Each of you is defending, not listening

When you feel accused, your brain switches from understanding to protecting. You both end up preparing your counter-argument while the other one talks.

3. The argument ends, but nothing is decided

Someone apologises to make it stop, or you both go quiet and it fades. Nothing changes, so the same situation produces the same fight next week.

4. Old fights get pulled in

"And another thing…" turns a ten-minute conversation into a trial of the whole relationship. Each new round carries the weight of every previous one.

5. The timing is always wrong

Tired, hungry, late at night, or in the middle of something. The topic only comes up when neither of you has any patience left.

How to find what the fight is really about

Each of you answers these on your own, then compare:

  • What usually starts it, and what do I feel in the first ten seconds?
  • What am I afraid it means about us?
  • What would I need to feel for this to stop bothering me?
  • What do I think my partner needs, in their words, not mine?

If you write the answers down, you'll often notice the same two needs showing up in every version of the fight.

How to break the loop

  1. Pick a calm time. Not in the middle of the next round. "Can we talk about the thing we keep fighting about, on Sunday morning?"
  2. Each of you names your need. Not the complaint: the need. "I need to feel like we're a team on the house." "I need to come home and breathe for ten minutes."
  3. Repeat back what you heard. Before replying, say their need back until they say "yes, that's it."
  4. Look for the overlap. Most recurring fights have a solution that meets both needs. You just can't see it while you're defending.
  5. Agree one specific step. Who does what, by when. "Dishes after dinner are mine on weekdays, and I get ten minutes when I come in."
  6. Check in a week later. Did it work? Adjust it. A perpetual problem needs maintenance, not a one-time fix.

Common questions

Why do couples keep having the same argument?

Because they argue about the surface topic instead of the need underneath it on each side. Until both needs are named and heard, solutions don't stick, and the same fight returns with a new trigger.

Is it normal to have the same fight over and over?

Yes. Research from the Gottman Institute suggests most relationship conflicts are perpetual, rooted in lasting differences. What matters is whether you can talk about them calmly and agree on ways to manage them.

How do you stop having the same argument?

Talk about it at a calm time, each name the need underneath, repeat back what you heard, agree one specific step, and check in a week later to adjust it.

When is a recurring argument a sign of something bigger?

When it involves contempt, threats or fear, when one person always gives in, or when nothing changes despite both of you trying. Then a couples counselor is worth considering.
